\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}

Four bodies of work bear on the question: per-request enforcement at a gateway, federated optimisation under heterogeneity, payload representation, and the security of the update channel. Statements below that something has not been done are limited to the searches described in the positioning subsection.

\subsection{Zero-Trust Architecture}
Zero trust drops the assumption that a request is benign because it originates inside the network perimeter. NIST SP~800-207 formalises this: no implicit trust is granted by network location, and every access decision is made per request, based on the observable state of the requester and the resource~\cite{nist800207}. Google's BeyondCorp was an early large-scale deployment of the idea~\cite{beyondcorp}. The architecture separates a policy decision point from policy enforcement points that sit in front of each resource.

Here the enforcement point is a gateway-resident Web Application Firewall (WAF), and the only decision signal is a learned score over the request payload. Requester identity and posture, which NIST assigns to the policy decision point, are outside the prototype; it supplies one input to such a decision. The open problem is how to keep that decision function current against new payloads without centralising the traffic it is trained on. Signature engines such as Snort~\cite{roesch1999} depend on curated rules and cannot adapt on their own. Sommer and Paxson also caution that learned detectors in network security cannot assume a closed world, because the benign class is site-specific and attacks keep changing~\cite{sommer2010}. That observation motivates testing the benign-traffic profile as a heterogeneity axis, separately from the attack mix.

\subsection{Federated Learning}
Federated learning (FL) trains a shared model while data stays on the participants. In Federated Averaging (FedAvg), each client runs local optimisation and the server averages the returned parameters, weighted by local sample count~\cite{mcmahan2017}. Kairouz \textit{et al.} survey the open problems~\cite{kairouz2021}; those most relevant here are statistical heterogeneity, communication cost, and adversarial participants.

\paragraph{Heterogeneity.}
When client distributions differ, local optima diverge. Li \textit{et al.} prove that FedAvg converges more slowly on non-IID data~\cite{li2020convergence}, and FedProx adds a proximal term to stabilise training under such heterogeneity~\cite{li2020fedprox}. The main comparison uses plain FedAvg and asks which \emph{kind} of heterogeneity helps or hurts it; FedProx appears only as a baseline (Section~\ref{sec:eval-baselines}).

\paragraph{Privacy.}
Raw data never leaves a client, but updates are not free of information: gradients can be inverted to reconstruct training inputs~\cite{zhu2019}. Differentially private training with clipping and noise~\cite{abadi2016} and cryptographic secure aggregation~\cite{bonawitz2017} are the standard mitigations. Mothukuri \textit{et al.} survey attacks and defences~\cite{mothukuri2021}.

\paragraph{Byzantine robustness.}
A compromised client can upload arbitrary parameters. Krum~\cite{blanchard2017}, coordinate-wise median and trimmed mean~\cite{yin2018} replace the mean with a robust statistic, under the assumption that honest clients outnumber malicious ones. These rules are not a complete defence: Fang \textit{et al.} construct local model-poisoning attacks that degrade them~\cite{fang2020}. Baruch \textit{et al.} show that small perturbations within the natural variance of honest updates evade Krum and median-style rules~\cite{baruch2019alie}, and Shejwalkar and Houmansadr give a generic optimisation framework for such attacks~\cite{shejwalkar2021manipulating}. Bulyan adds a second filtering stage because coordinate-wise rules leave a margin that grows with dimension~\cite{elmhamdi2018bulyan}. FLTrust takes a different route: the server trains on a small clean root dataset and weights client updates by cosine similarity to its own update~\cite{cao2021fltrust}. This suits an aggregator that holds an anchor set; we do not implement it. Shejwalkar \textit{et al.} argue that the threat models in much of this literature (large fractions of compromised clients, omniscient attackers) are unrealistic for production FL~\cite{shejwalkar2022drawingboard}; our small deployments of enrolled gateways are closer to a cross-silo setting where a few compromised peers are plausible. Our simulation (Section~\ref{sec:eval-byzantine}) uses five fixed attacks, including ALIE and the Fang attack, none adapted to the particular aggregator beyond what those attacks prescribe, so it supports the defence mechanics at the stated fractions and not robustness against adaptive adversaries.

\paragraph{Communication.}
Update size dominates cost on constrained links. Quantisation and encoding schemes such as QSGD reduce it~\cite{alistarh2017}. A fixed-width feature map attacks the same problem differently, by making the model, and hence the update, a constant size.

\paragraph{FL for IoT security.}
FL has been applied to anomaly detection on IoT gateways, for example DIoT, which learns per-device-type communication profiles federatedly~\cite{nguyen2019diot}, and is surveyed for IoT in general by Nguyen \textit{et al.}~\cite{nguyen2021}. Fed-IIoT trains a federated Android-malware detector for industrial IoT and defends the aggregation step against GAN-based poisoning~\cite{taheri2021fediiot}. These systems classify network flows or application binaries, not HTTP request payloads.

\paragraph{Federated web-attack detection.}
The closest work on our task is federated XSS detection. Jazi and Ben-Gal report that a federated model matches centralised models while keeping data local~\cite{jazi2024fedxss}. Wang \textit{et al.} study out-of-distribution and non-IID client splits for federated XSS detection with different text embeddings~\cite{wang2025fedxss}. FedXSS-Guard combines a transformer encoder, a BiLSTM and adversarial training, and reports sub-5\,ms latency on server, edge and mobile hardware~\cite{bharath2026fedxssguard}. All three target XSS only and use learned embeddings or deep models, and their accuracy figures are self-reported and not reproduced here. We cover SQLi and XSS with a fixed-width hashed $n$-gram linear model and separate attack-campaign heterogeneity from benign-profile heterogeneity; we did not compare against these models empirically and make no claim of superiority. In the searches described in the positioning subsection we found no federated SQLi or federated WAF study, which is not proof of absence.

\subsection{Behavioural and \texorpdfstring{$n$}{n}-gram Profiling of Payloads}
Behavioural profiling models what normal input looks like and scores deviations. Forrest \textit{et al.} used short sequences of system calls as a signature of ``self'' for processes~\cite{forrest1996}. For network and web traffic, PAYL models the byte-frequency distribution of payloads~\cite{wang2004payl}, and Kruegel and Vigna model request attributes of web applications~\cite{kruegel2003}. Character $n$-gram statistics are a long-standing representation in text classification~\cite{cavnar1994}, and they transfer directly to HTTP payloads because injection attacks have distinctive local character patterns (for example quote--keyword--comment sequences in SQL injection, or angle-bracket--tag sequences in XSS).

An explicit $n$-gram vocabulary grows with the traffic seen, which would make the model size, and every synchronisation payload, unbounded. The hashing trick maps $n$-grams into a fixed number of buckets without a vocabulary~\cite{weinberger2009}. Our feature map hashes character trigrams ($n=3$) into 256 buckets with a polynomial rolling hash, $\ell_2$-normalises the bucket counts, and appends three scaled syntactic scalars (length, count of non-alphanumeric characters, Shannon entropy), giving a 259-dimensional vector (\texttt{fedzta\_features.py}). The same module runs on cloud and edge so both sides bucket identically. Because the map is fixed and cheap to compute, a linear classifier suffices on the gateway. Hashed $n$-gram payload models are also known to be evadable by an attacker who shapes the byte statistics of an attack~\cite{fogla2006}, a limitation the linear model inherits and this study does not test.

\paragraph{Prior payload detectors and evasion.}
Our feature design is closest to the statistical payload models above. Anagram models higher-order $n$-grams in Bloom filters and randomises which parts of a packet are modelled to resist mimicry~\cite{wang2006anagram}, and McPAD combines several one-class classifiers over $2_v$-gram features of the payload~\cite{perdisci2009mcpad}. Both are centralised, unsupervised or one-class detectors for network payloads; ours is a supervised linear model over hashed trigrams, trained federatedly. This family has a known weakness: polymorphic blending attacks pad or re-encode an exploit so that its byte statistics match the normal profile and evade PAYL-style models~\cite{fogla2006pba}. A hashed trigram profile with a linear decision function is a statistical payload model in this sense, so we expect it to be susceptible to blending-style evasion; input-space evasion is not evaluated.

\subsection{Post-Quantum Key Encapsulation for the Weight Channel}
Model parameters are valuable, and an adversary who records the channel today may decrypt it later. Shor's algorithm breaks the factoring and discrete-logarithm problems that underlie RSA and elliptic-curve Diffie--Hellman~\cite{shor1997}, so key exchange that relies on them is exposed to this ``store now, decrypt later'' strategy. Post-quantum cryptography replaces them with problems believed hard for quantum computers~\cite{bernstein2017}.

NIST has standardised two lattice-based schemes derived from the CRYSTALS submissions. \textbf{ML-KEM} (FIPS~203)~\cite{fips203}, derived from CRYSTALS-Kyber~\cite{bos2018kyber}, is a key-encapsulation mechanism: the receiver publishes a public key, the sender encapsulates a fresh shared secret under it and returns a ciphertext, and the receiver decapsulates. The shared secret then keys a symmetric cipher for the payload. \textbf{ML-DSA} (FIPS~204)~\cite{fips204}, derived from CRYSTALS-Dilithium~\cite{ducas2018dilithium}, is the corresponding digital signature scheme.

The prototype uses ML-KEM-512, the smallest parameter set, only as a standalone benchmark, and authenticates updates with a symmetric MAC instead of a signature (Sections~\ref{sec:sys-auth} and~\ref{sec:sys-pqc}). ML-DSA would replace the shared secret with per-gateway public keys at the price of an extra edge dependency and is not evaluated here.

\subsection{Positioning}
The search behind the novelty statements in this paper was bounded: a small number of web and preprint searches on federated WAF and web-attack detection, post-quantum federated learning, and Byzantine-robust aggregation, plus the authors' knowledge of the literature. It was not a systematic review, and absence of a hit means only that none was found. Within that bound, each ingredient exists elsewhere: federated detection of injection attacks, hashed $n$-gram payload models, robust aggregation, and post-quantum key exchange on small devices, with recent preprints combining post-quantum protection and Byzantine-robust federated learning for industrial intrusion detection. The measurement we found no counterpart for is the controlled comparison of benign-profile and attack-mix heterogeneity for a payload classifier under unique-payload-disjoint evaluation, and the care needed for that comparison follows the general warnings on dataset construction in security machine learning~\cite{arp2022}. The robustness we examine is that of the median and trimmed-mean rules, in simulation, against five fixed attack strategies (Section~\ref{sec:eval-byzantine}), and no formal privacy guarantee is claimed.

Three observations follow. First, federated XSS detection exists~\cite{jazi2024fedxss,wang2025fedxss,bharath2026fedxssguard}, so we do not claim to be first on federated web-attack detection. Second, post-quantum Byzantine-robust FL for industrial IoT intrusion detection has been proposed as well~\cite{rahmati2026pqbyz,nayak2026pqfl}, both as preprints whose claims we have not verified; our post-quantum component is a standalone ML-KEM benchmark outside the training loop, so the paper is not positioned as a post-quantum system. Third, the aggregation rules we test are standard~\cite{yin2018,blanchard2017}, and stronger attacks and defences exist~\cite{baruch2019alie,fang2020,cao2021fltrust,elmhamdi2018bulyan}.
